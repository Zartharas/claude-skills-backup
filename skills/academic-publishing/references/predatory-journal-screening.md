# Predatory Journal / Venue Screening Checklist

Use this before recommending or submitting to any venue the researcher hasn't
published in before. A single strong paper in a bad venue can cost more
credibility than the paper gains — and it's very hard to undo after publication.

## Verify independently, don't trust the journal's own claims

- **Index membership**: check DOAJ (doaj.org), Scopus, Web of Science, or the
  field's disciplinary index *directly* — via `web_search` — rather than trusting
  a badge/logo displayed on the journal's own website. Badges are trivially
  faked.
- **Editorial board**: are the named editors real, findable, affiliated where
  claimed? Search a few names independently. A board of real academics who don't
  know they're listed is a known predatory pattern.
- **Publisher reputation**: search the publisher's name plus "predatory" — this
  surfaces community discussion (Retraction Watch, field forums, librarian
  resources) faster than most other methods.
- **ISSN**: confirm it resolves to the actual journal via the ISSN portal, not
  just displayed on the page.

## Red flags

- **Unrealistic turnaround**: genuine peer review takes weeks to months
  depending on field norms; "accepted in 3 days" for a journal claiming rigorous
  peer review is disqualifying.
- **Fee opacity**: article processing charges (APCs) not disclosed until after
  acceptance, or aggressive fee-focused solicitation emails.
- **Scope that's implausibly broad**: a single journal claiming top-tier coverage
  across unrelated fields is a common template pattern.
- **Solicitation over submission**: unsolicited emails inviting submission,
  especially referencing a loosely related paper of the researcher's, are a
  known predatory-journal acquisition channel — legitimate journals rarely cold-
  solicit this way.
- **Impact factor claims that don't check out**: search the claimed metric
  independently (Clarivate JCR, Scimago) — some predatory venues cite a
  self-created or unaffiliated "impact factor."
- **No clear retraction/correction policy**, no COPE (Committee on Publication
  Ethics) membership where the field would expect it.

## Legitimate but non-obvious cases — don't over-flag these

- New, small, or field-specific journals aren't automatically predatory — check
  the criteria above rather than treating unfamiliarity as disqualifying.
- Open-access is not itself a red flag; most reputable journals now have OA
  options or are OA-native. The red flag is the *combination* of OA + fee
  opacity + no real review process, not OA alone.
- Regional or non-English-language journals may legitimately have lower
  international visibility without being predatory — verify via the checks
  above rather than visibility as a proxy for legitimacy.

## Before final submission decision

1. Confirm current standing (quartile/ranking) via `web_search` against
   Scimago or JCR — don't rely on memorized rankings, they go stale.
2. Confirm scope fit: does this journal actually publish work like this paper,
   based on recent issues, not just its stated scope?
3. Confirm the target journal's policy on prior preprint posting if the paper
   was posted to arXiv/SSRN/etc. — this varies and can affect eligibility.
4. If genuinely uncertain after all checks, that uncertainty itself is worth
   surfacing rather than resolving silently in either direction.
