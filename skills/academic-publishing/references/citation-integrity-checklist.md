# Citation Integrity Checklist

Run this for every citation before it goes in a draft. This is the checklist
version of the discipline described in the main skill — use it as a literal
per-citation gate, not a one-time read.

## Before citing anything

1. **Locate the actual source.** Use `web_search` to find it, `web_fetch` to read
   it. Training-data familiarity with a paper's existence or general topic is not
   the same as having read the specific passage that supports your claim.
2. **Find the specific passage**, not just the paper. A citation should point to
   the part of the source that actually makes the claim, not the paper's general
   vicinity.
3. **Check the claim matches the source's claim**, not just the source's topic.
   "Smith (2023) found X" requires Smith (2023) to actually report finding X —
   not merely to discuss the subject X falls under.
4. **Check direction and certainty match.** If the source says "may contribute to"
   and the draft says "causes," that's a misrepresentation even with the right
   citation attached.
5. **If it can't be verified, mark it, don't cite it.** Use `[COULD NOT VERIFY —
   claim needs a different source or should be softened/removed]` inline rather
   than citing something unconfirmed or dropping the claim silently.

## Red flags in your own drafting

- A related-work paragraph that reads like a list ("A found X. B found Y. C found
  Z.") instead of a synthesis — often a sign sources were collected but not
  actually read closely enough to compare.
- A citation count that seems to exist mainly to look thorough rather than because
  each source is doing argumentative work.
- Citing a review/survey paper for a specific empirical claim that the original
  study made — cite the primary source when the claim is specific.
- Multiple citations bundled at the end of a sentence with no clarity on which
  supports which part of the claim.
- Your own prior work cited more than the argument requires (self-citation should
  earn its place the same as any other source).

## When synthesizing across many sources

- Group by theme, tension, or finding — not by author/year in sequence.
- State explicitly where sources agree, where they conflict, and where the
  literature is silent (the actual gap).
- If two sources seem to disagree, verify it's a real disagreement (different
  populations, methods, definitions) and not an artifact of your summary.

## Field-specific verification sources (use web_search to reach these)

- General: Google Scholar, the journal's own site, publisher pages (Elsevier,
  Springer, Wiley, etc.)
- Preprints: arXiv, SSRN, bioRxiv, medRxiv — check whether the cited version is
  the preprint or a later peer-reviewed version, and cite accordingly
- DOI resolution: doi.org — confirms a DOI is real and points where claimed

This list is a floor, not a ceiling — the standard is: could you defend this
citation to an editor who pulled up the source themselves?
